3. Explanations Behind the Script

Chain algorithm: detailed explanation

The problem. Four players must feel physically tied together: they can't wander off, a puller drags the others, and it has to be cheap and stable for a multiplayer server. Three pieces split the job. Verlet moves the rope, Iterative Constraint Satisfaction keeps its segments the right length, and MACO decides who slows down when the rope is stretched. A fourth piece, the client limit, is the hard stop on separation.

Settings (ChainConfig). 12 segments (13 points) per link, 18 studs total, so 1.5 studs per segment. 20 constraint passes, gravity −80, damping 0.97, point radius 0.35, 30 updates per second, replicated every update.

Each update, on the server. The loop collects time into a fixed 1/30 s step (a single frame's time is capped at 0.1 s, so a lag spike can't blow up the physics). For each of the 3 links it runs Verlet and then the 20 passes. After all links it runs MACO, then sends the rope points to every client. The whole thing is timed for the 16.6 ms budget.

Verlet, in detail. Each interior point keeps only its current and previous position. Its velocity is implied by the difference, so new = pos + (pos − prev) × 0.97 + gravity × dt². Damping at 0.97 bleeds a little energy each step so the rope settles instead of swinging forever. Gravity is −80, stronger than Roblox's default, so the rope hangs convincingly. Before a point moves, a raycast checks the path. If it would pass through a wall, the point is placed on the surface, pushed out by its 0.35 radius. The two end points are not simulated. They are pinned to the players' real positions every update. Why Verlet: no stored velocity to go unstable, simple, and cheap enough for 36 moving points at 30 Hz.

Iterative Constraint Satisfaction, in detail. After Verlet moves the points, segments are the wrong length. A pass looks at each neighboring pair, measures the gap, and moves both points halfway toward 1.5 studs apart (the pinned end points stay put). Example: a gap of 2.5 has an error of 1.0, so each point moves 0.5 and the gap becomes 1.5. Moving one pair disturbs the next pair, so one pass is not enough. Repeating 20 times lets the corrections spread along the rope until it settles. More passes make the rope stiffer but cost more time, so 20 is a balance against the 16.6 ms budget. The ends are re-pinned after every pass.

MACO, in detail. For each link, the server takes the distance between the two real players and the link's limit (18, or 8 for the middle link in a four-player chain). Slowing can only start when the link is past 65% of its limit. Progress is t = (distance − 0.65 × limit) / (0.35 × limit), from 0 to 1. Two more conditions must hold. First, the player must be moving away from the neighbor (the dot product of their move direction with the link direction beyond 0.2). Second, at least one more link behind them must also be taut, because the slowdown counts how many players they are dragging. Then penalty = tautLinks × 0.55 × t and speed = base × (1 − penalty), never below 25% of base (8 when walking, 12 when sprinting). Sprinting multiplies the 0.55 cost by 0.35. Players moving toward a neighbor are never slowed. Examples with the outer link at 14.4 studs (t = 0.43): one taut link behind gives 32 → about 24.5; two give 32 → about 16.9; a sprinter with one taut link behind goes 48 → about 44. Why this design: the player who pulls pays, everyone else can always catch up, and the slowdown grows smoothly with no sudden stop.

Client limit, in detail. Roblox lets each client control its own character, so the hard stop has to run on the device. Within the last 3 studs of the limit, each frame removes 12% of the outward speed, scaled by how deep in the zone the player is. If the player is past the limit by more than 0.3 studs, they are nudged back by 20% of the overshoot, after a wall check so they aren't pushed into geometry. It switches off while a Pull is happening.

Rendering. Clients interpolate between server updates. The drawn rope's end points always follow the real players, and each link piece is stretched to the live gap with a 25% overlap to hide seams. Below 85% stretch, the drawn rope is blended toward a straight line to hide Verlet wiggle. Close to taut, you see the real simulated shape.

Honest trade-off. The server owns the rope and the speed penalty, but the final stop on separation is client-side. That is fine for a research prototype with four cooperative testers, and the thesis already documents it.

Chain algorithm: short version

  • Verlet: new = pos + (pos − prev) × 0.97 + gravity × dt². Ends pinned to players. Cheap and stable at 30 Hz.
  • Iterative Constraint Satisfaction: 20 passes. Example: two points 2.5 apart have an error of 1.0, so each moves 0.5 and they end at 1.5.
  • MACO: t = (stretch − 0.65 × limit) / (0.35 × limit). If the player moves away and at least one more link is taut, penalty = tautLinks × 0.55 × t, speed = base × (1 − penalty), floor 25%. Example: outer link at 14.4 studs gives t = 0.43. With one more taut link, walking 32 becomes about 24.5, and sprinting 48 becomes about 44.
  • Client limit: last 3 studs bleed 12% of outward speed per frame, scaled by depth. Past the limit by 0.3 studs, a nudge of 20% of the overshoot, with a wall check.

Calibration Metrics overlay: detailed explanation

What it is and why it exists. A display-only panel (top-right "Calibration Metrics" button). Objective 5 requires calibrating and verifying the algorithms with measured values, and this panel shows them live. Four server scripts feed it: ChainMetricsService (5 times a second), LatencyMonitor, ChainManager's debug feed (once a second) and SnakeBossServer (4 times a second). The client computes nothing except reporting its own position.

① Verlet / ICS. Shows: constraint error ε (current, average, max), passes used out of 20, peak-load ε, timestep drift at 30/60/120 fps, solver cost in µs. Why: proves the rope stays near its set length and fits the time budget. Answers: Constraint Error, Per-Update Computation Time, TC-01, TC-02. Thresholds: convergence means a pass got segment error under 25%, solver cost passes under 16,600 µs. Caution: ε is measured per 1.5-stud segment, not per whole link as the thesis defines it, so call it a calibration reading.

② MACO. Shows: tension per link, stress spread, near-failure events (a link at 90% or more of its limit, so 16.2 studs on an outer link), time to stabilize. Why: shows how stretched the chain gets and how evenly. Answers: TC-03. Caution: the 8-stud middle link can never reach 16.2, so it never counts an event, the spread is in raw studs, and the "MACO off" counter stays at zero because nothing switches MACO off yet.

③ Shared failure. Shows: propagation latency, false positives and negatives. Answers: TC-04. Caution: these only update if ChainDeathManager calls the recording functions, which I haven't seen, so check it before showing this section.

④ Predictive Pathfinding. Shows: prediction accuracy (within 10 studs), interception rate, boss state, lookahead, the last prediction error. Why: measures how good the boss's forecast is. In the world, a yellow ball marks the prediction and a white ball marks the player's real position. Answers: Trajectory Prediction Error, TC-06.

⑤ Network. Shows: the distance in studs between the server's position and the client's reported position. Why: shows how well the game stays in sync.

⑥ Latency. Shows: ping per player rated Good (100 ms or less), Fair (200 ms or less) or Poor, round trip time, and a warning when one player's connection is much worse than the rest. Why: a laggy player may see delayed speed corrections. Answers: Network Latency.

⑦ System debug. Shows: session state and restarts, Pull requests (accepted, declined, timed out, average accept time), total frame time vs solver-only time as a share of 16.6 ms, chain topology, and a collision-group check. Why: shows whether a frame drop is the algorithm's fault or something else in the same update. Answers: Time Behaviour, Resource Utilization, Capacity.

Reading it live. Green [PASS] means within the current threshold. Frame rate is not here, because the thesis takes it from Roblox's built-in statistics.

Snake Boss predictive AI

  • Smooths each player's velocity, then picks the nearest living player within 95 studs (switches only if another is 15 studs nearer).
  • lookahead = distance / 17, clamped to 0.15–1.5 s, so the cap is reached at about 26 studs. predicted = position + velocity × lookahead. Example: 10 studs away gives 0.59 s.
  • Pathfinding goes to the forecast, replanning at most every 0.4 s. Attacks need the target and forecast within 30 studs, a 2 s cooldown, and aim locks 0.25 s early so it can be dodged.
  • Every 0.2 s it stores a prediction and later checks it against the real position (within 10 studs counts as accurate). Every attack counts as an interception attempt.
  • Why: the boss moves at 17 and players at 32 or 48, so it must lead the target. Beyond the thesis: it is a state machine tied to the altars (a lit altar stuns it 2 s, four lit kills it, a wrong totem order respawns it).

Puzzle Metrics panel

  • Read-only view (PUZZLE button or F4) of in-game logging: hint items found, Zone 1 totems (immediate recall), Zone 3 totems (delayed recall within the session), first-attempt correctness, attempts, time to solve, per-player clicks.
  • Matches the thesis's supporting data: task completion time, error rates, puzzle attempts. It does not replace the Corsi and matrix reasoning pre-test and post-test.
  • Reset metrics is open to every player, so don't press it during testing. I haven't verified how the chance-of-first-attempt figure is computed.

4. Likely Questions and Ready Answers

Algorithm questions

Why Verlet instead of Roblox's rope or physics? "Built-in ropes link two players and give us no control over the speed rule or measurement. Verlet is simple, stable, and cheap enough for four players at 30 updates a second, and we can measure every step."

Why 20 passes? "It is a balance between rope stiffness and the 16.6 millisecond time budget. We calibrate it with the solver cost and convergence readings." (Don't say it was mathematically derived.)

Is MACO really optimization? "No. It is a rule-based speed penalty. The name comes from the multi-agent constraint optimization field, and no cost function is minimized. The thesis states this."

Why 65%, 0.55 and the 25% floor? "They are tuned values from playtesting. Our limitations already say the chain relies on manual tuning, and the calibration metrics are how we check them."

What if two players pull in opposite directions? "Each one counts as a puller on their side, so each is slowed if more taut links are behind them. The limit is never broken, because the client stop still applies."

Why is the limit enforced on the client? Can it be bypassed? "Roblox clients control their own characters, so the stop has to run there. The server still simulates the rope, applies the penalty and measures. A modified client could bypass it in principle, which is an acceptable limitation for a research prototype with cooperative testers."

How do you know the chain is stable? "The panel reports constraint error, passes needed and solver cost, and the test cases TC-01 and TC-02 check the Verlet math by hand and the error after the passes."

Why 30 updates and not 60? "It keeps the server cost and network traffic low, and the clients smooth between updates. We measure the per-update cost against the 16.6 millisecond budget to confirm it fits."

Why does the boss predict, and what are the limits? "Players move at 32 to 48 and the boss at 17, so chasing the current position always lags. It projects the player's position forward by a lookahead of 0.15 to 1.5 seconds. It assumes steady movement, so sudden turns increase the error. We measure that error."

Paper questions

What is your research gap? "No reviewed work combines chain constraints, a speed-penalty rule and predictive enemy AI in one Roblox game, or pairs them with a measured cognitive outcome."

Why combine three algorithms? "Each does a different job: Verlet moves the rope, Iterative Constraint Satisfaction keeps it within length, and MACO keeps pulling fair. Together they make four tethered players behave coherently."

How will you measure cognitive change? "A block-sequence task based on the Corsi Block Test for short-term memory, and a matrix reasoning task for generalization, taken before and after play with separate forms. Both are kept separate from the in-game puzzles."

Why the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test? "It compares two related sets of scores, before and after, and does not assume a normal distribution, which suits a small sample. We use α = 0.05."

How many participants? "At least 32 players, in eight groups of four, because the game needs four players. One expert evaluator has been engaged so far."

Can you claim the game improves memory without a control group? "No. We claim only a measurable short-term change within the evaluation sessions. We do not claim long-term retention, intelligence or clinical effects, and we list that as a limitation."

Why only Performance Efficiency from ISO/IEC 25010:2023? "It covers Time Behaviour, Resource Utilization and Capacity, which match what a chained multiplayer game stresses most. Other characteristics are outside our scope."

Why Roblox, adults 18+ and Metro Manila? "Roblox provides multiplayer, PC and mobile play and Luau scripting, so effort goes into the chain and enemy systems. The 18+ and Metro Manila limits are our stated scope."

How do you know the history content is accurate? "The portals draw on documented sources reviewed in Chapter II, and a history professor from UMak is the expert evaluator."

What is finished so far? "The chain, the speed penalty, the client limit, Sprint, Pull and the calibration panel run today. Remaining work is content and evaluation." Adjust to your real status.

5. Cautions and Quick Answers

  • Constraint error: the thesis defines it per whole link, but the code measures each 1.5-stud segment. Treat the number as a calibration reading and don't lead with it.
  • Middle link: its 8-stud limit means it never reaches the near-failure threshold, so it never counts a peak-stress event.
  • Don't claim a Stretch Reduction Rate percentage or that the predictive boss beats a reactive one. Say "those comparison runs are part of the testing phase."
  • Demo setup: use four players (the code allows 2 for testing), and skip the slingshot launch, which is not in the thesis.
  • Why a chain? No one moves alone, so the physics creates cooperation.
  • Why not Roblox's rope? It only links two players and gives no control over the speed rule or measurement.
  • Is MACO optimization? It is a rule-based speed penalty. The name is borrowed, and no cost function is minimized.
  • A panel shows [FAIL]? The metrics are for calibration and some thresholds are still being tuned.
